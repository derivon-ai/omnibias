# Distributed residual programs: data-parallel Gram, Gauss-Newton and NTK diagonal

The structured residual programs shard their points over a device mesh, keep the weights replicated and exchange compact
stream factors instead of Jacobian rows. The sharded program computes the same numbers as the single-device program up to
rounding; what this page measures is how much faster it is, on how many devices, and where it stops getting faster. The
planner front ends of both back ends (`omnibias.jax.operators`, `omnibias.torch.operators`) export
`distributed_residual_gram`, `distributed_residual_gauss_newton` and `distributed_residual_ntk_diagonal` (JAX: over a
`jax.sharding.Mesh`; PyTorch: over `torch.distributed`, NCCL on GPUs).

## What is divided and what is not

For `N` residual rows the Gram matrix `K = J J^T` has `N x N` entries.

- The forward-Laplacian streams of each device's own points are computed locally: the whole forward pass is divided by the
  device count.
- For every hidden layer the devices all-gather the input streams and the output-stream cotangents of the other devices'
  points (`O(N T (in + out))` numbers per layer, against the `N^2` of `K`) and fill their own rows of `K` from the stream
  identity, at `1/n_dev` of the arithmetic.
- The damped solve `(K + damping I) alpha = r` runs on the gathered, replicated `K`. Its memory (`N^2` per device) and its
  arithmetic (`N^3 / 3`) are **not** divided by the device count. This is the term that stops the Gauss-Newton step from
  scaling, and the tables show it.
- A residual must act pointwise and depend on the point only through its `x` argument; every block's point count must be a
  multiple of the device count (`pad_block` pads with exact zero rows); the projected program for `D > H` has no distributed
  version.

```python
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
import numpy as np
from jax.sharding import Mesh

jax.config.update("jax_enable_x64", True)

from omnibias.jax.experimental import deep_residual_gram
from omnibias.jax.operators import distributed_residual_gram, shard_points

mesh = Mesh(np.array(jax.devices()), ("data",))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
D, H = 5, 6
sizes, acts = [D, H, H, 1], ["tanh", "tanh", None]
layers = [
    (jnp.asarray(rng.standard_normal((b, a)) / np.sqrt(a)), jnp.asarray(0.1 * rng.standard_normal(b)), acts[i])
    for i, (a, b) in enumerate(zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:]))
]
X = jnp.asarray(rng.standard_normal((8, D)))  # 8 rows: a multiple of 1, 2, 4 and 8 devices
interior = lambda u, g, lap, x: lap[:, 0] + 0.5 * u[:, 0] - jnp.sin(x[:, 0])

r1, K1 = deep_residual_gram([(X, interior, 2)], layers)  # one device
r2, K2 = distributed_residual_gram([(shard_points(X, mesh), interior, 2)], layers, mesh)  # the mesh
np.testing.assert_allclose(np.asarray(K2), np.asarray(K1), rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
```

Each function compiles its sharded program on every call from eager code; call it under your own `jax.jit` inside a
training step so that the program is built once.

## Measured

One host with four A100-SXM4-80GB (NVLink topology not recorded), float64, a tanh MLP with `D = 16`, width 64 and three hidden
layers. The residual is a second-order interior block plus a value boundary block of a quarter of the rows. Each timing is
the median of five calls after a first call that compiles; the compile time is in the records. The sharded Gauss-Newton step
agrees with the single-device step to a relative `5e-10` or better (largest measured: 4.4e-10). The tables are generated by
`omnibias_experiments/multigpu_20261022/make_tables.py` from the committed records and are not edited.

## How to read these numbers

- **The Gram matrix scales, the Gauss-Newton step scales less.** At 16,384 rows the solve and step take about 101 ms at one,
  two and four devices (the solve is replicated), which is a floor under the Gauss-Newton step: 192 ms at four devices. The
  Gram matrix alone reaches 3.1x on four devices in JAX and 3.0x in PyTorch at that size, and less at 10,240 rows (2.6x,
  2.7x) where fixed costs are a larger share.
- **Weak scaling is not flat for the Gram matrix**, and should not be: with a fixed number of rows per device the total
  number of rows grows with the device count and `K` has `N^2` entries. The weak-scaling table reports times, not an
  efficiency, for that reason. The point-parallel Laplacian has no `N^2` term and keeps 81% efficiency in JAX (forward only)
  and 98% in PyTorch (forward, backward and a gradient all-reduce) on four devices.
- **PyTorch and JAX differ in what is timed.** The JAX operator rows are a forward Laplacian without a parameter
  gradient. The PyTorch rows are a training step: forward, backward to the weights and a manual all-reduce of the gradients
  (no `DistributedDataParallel` bucket overlap). They are not a comparison of the two frameworks.
- The speedups divide a single-device time measured in the same job by the multi-device time. They say nothing about how the
  single-device program compares with other methods; see [performance](performance.md) for that.

## Not measured

- More than four devices. An eight-device job was submitted on 22 Oct 09:55 PDT and had not started 15.7 hours later (23 Oct 01:36 PDT), when it was
  cancelled; no eight-device number is reported or inferred from the four-device ones.
- More than one host, or any interconnect other than the one inside that host. All-gather volume grows with the device count and
  the cost of it across hosts is unmeasured.
- A100 only. The page says nothing about other GPU models, and TPU is unmeasured.
- Training convergence with the sharded step. These are step times and agreement with the single-device step, not an end-to-end
  training comparison; the end-to-end suite (`omnibias_experiments/e2e_20261022`) trains on one device.
